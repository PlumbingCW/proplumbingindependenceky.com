Hydro jetting sends water through a hose at several thousand pounds per square inch, with nozzle jets that cut forward and scrub backward. It clears what a cable only punches through. Below, we explain when jetting makes sense, when it does not, and how the job runs inside a Kenton County home.

Drains that clog again every few months

A line that opens with a cable, then slows again in weeks, is telling you something. The cable cut a hole through the middle of the blockage and left the walls coated. Grease, soap scale, and fine roots stay attached to the pipe, and new debris catches on them.

That pattern is what hydro jetting is built for. Water pressure scours the full circle of the pipe, not just the center. If your sink, tub, or main line keeps returning to the same slow state, ask for jetting after a camera look. For one-off clogs, our drain cleaning service is usually the quicker, simpler choice.

If you are not sure which situation you have, a short camera visit through our sewer inspection service can settle it. The footage shows whether the walls are coated, rooted, or broken, so you pay for the right fix the first time. Landlords with several units on one stack often choose jetting for this reason.

How a jetting rig does the work

  1. Camera first

    A small camera goes down the line to show the pipe material, the clog, and any cracks. Jetting a broken pipe can make things worse, so this look comes before any pressure.

  2. Hose and nozzle feed in

    The hose enters through a cleanout. A nozzle with rear-facing jets pulls the hose forward as it works, while a forward jet breaks the blockage apart.

  3. Pressure is set to the pipe

    Cast iron, PVC, and clay each tolerate different pressure. The technician starts lower and increases only as the pipe and the clog allow.

  4. Slow pull back

    Jets scrub the wall on the way out, flushing debris toward the main. Several passes over the worst stretch are normal.

  5. Second camera pass

    A follow-up view confirms the walls are clean and shows anything the jetting exposed, such as a cracked joint or a root entry point.

What the technician checks before turning on the pressure

The jetting itself takes less time than the checks around it. These are the points that decide whether it is safe.

  • ✓Pipe material at the cleanout: cast iron, PVC, clay, or galvanized steel.
  • ✓Camera footage showing cracks, offset joints, or a sagging section.
  • ✓The distance from the cleanout to the clog, measured in feet.
  • ✓Whether a cable already got stuck, and where it stopped.
  • ✓Whether the line also serves a basement bath or floor drain below street level.
  • ✓Where the water and debris will exit, so the downstream line is clear.
  • ✓Whether the pipe is 2, 3, 4, or 6 inches, which sets the nozzle size.

When jetting is the wrong call

A good plumber says no to jetting when it would cause harm. These are the situations where we choose a different path.

Collapsed or crushed pipe

Pressure has nowhere to go and can push debris into the break. A repair comes first.

Badly corroded cast iron

Thin, flaking walls can give way under high pressure. We use gentler tools and a camera.

Offset joints

A lip where two pipes meet catches debris again after jetting. The joint needs repair.

A single soft clog

A cable does the job faster and costs less. Jetting would be more than the problem needs.

Bellied pipe holding water

A low spot refills with waste after any cleaning. Jetting only buys time until the sag is fixed.

What drives the cost of a jetting job

Jetting costs more than a basic cable pass because the rig, the water supply, and the time are larger. The final number depends on what we find in the pipe.

Length of line to clean

Jetting 30 feet of branch is quick. A full run to the street takes longer and uses more water.

Pipe size and condition

Larger pipe needs bigger nozzles. Fragile pipe needs slower, careful passes.

Camera passes

Cameras before and after add time but show you what was removed and what remains.

Whole-house or main only

Cleaning only the main is quicker than also jetting kitchen, bath, and laundry branches.

Access and scheduling

A missing cleanout adds labor, and after-hours calls follow different scheduling than weekday visits.

Insurance often covers sudden damage from a sewage backup, but rarely covers preventive jetting or slow buildup. Read your own policy and ask about water backup coverage before you need it.
