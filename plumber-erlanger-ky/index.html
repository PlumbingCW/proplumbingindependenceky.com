On May 31, 1887, the Erlanger Land Syndicate recorded a subdivision of 220 lots. Decades later, after Interstate 75 opened in the early 1960s, new subdivisions spread along the highway and Erlanger led the Cincinnati area in new construction for three years. Houses from those two moments have very different pipes.

Knowing your era tells us where to look. An old lot may hide worn drains and aging supply lines. A highway-boom house may be dealing with a water heater or a drain line that has finally reached the end of its useful life.

Reading your house by the decade it came from

Plumbing materials followed the building trends of their time. A house on one of the original syndicate lots may have been plumbed in an earlier century and updated in pieces, so its pipes may be a mix of old and newer materials. A house from the 1960s boom may have more uniform lines that are simply old.

We say may because we never judge a pipe by the address alone. A previous owner might have replaced the whole supply side in recent years. A technician checks what is actually behind the wall, looks at the water meter and pressure, and tells you what is real.

If you do not know the year your house was built, check the deed, the county records, or ask a neighbor. Even a rough decade helps dispatch plan.

The turnpike, the railroad, and the lots between them

Erlanger grew where roads and rails crossed. The Covington-Lexington turnpike was chartered in 1829, the Southern Railroad arrived in 1873, and the post office and depot took the name Erlanger in 1882. The 1887 syndicate lots filled in around those routes, with the city incorporating in 1897.

For a homeowner, the lesson is practical. The lots recorded in 1887 sit in the older part of town, around streets like Crescent Avenue, Center Street, and Hulbert Avenue. If your home is in that older core and the sewer line has never been scoped, a camera visit is a sensible first step.

If a pipe fails in an Erlanger house tonight

Start with the water, then the electricity. A supply pipe that has let go can pour out gallons in a few minutes, and an older house may have its main valve in an awkward spot like a crawlspace or behind a wall panel.

Call the 24/7 line even before you find the valve. We will stay on the phone and walk you toward it.

  1. Find the main valve, which is often near the meter or where the pipe enters the foundation, and turn it clockwise until it stops.
  2. Open the lowest faucet and flush the toilet once to drain what remains in the lines.
  3. If water is dripping near light fixtures or outlets, switch off those circuits at the panel.
  4. Lift small belongings, rugs, and boxes off the wet floor.
  5. Call the 24/7 line and tell dispatch the age of the house and where the water is coming from.
