The pit is filling and the pump is not running

A sump pit that fills faster than it empties gives you minutes to hours before water reaches finished floors. Call us first, then act on the list below. If the pump is plugged in but silent, the cause is often a stuck float, a tripped breaker or a burned-out motor.

Do not wade into water if the pump, outlet or nearby appliances are powered. Standing water and electricity can be deadly, so stay out and call a licensed electrician or us if you cannot cut power safely.

  1. Check that the pump is plugged in and that the outlet's reset button has not tripped.
  2. Lift the float by hand (only if no water covers any live cord). If the pump starts, the float is the problem.
  3. Use a bucket or wet vacuum to lower the pit level and buy time.
  4. Move boxes, rugs and stored items off the floor.
  5. Keep downspouts pointed away from the house while you wait.

Signs a sump pump is on its way out

Pumps rarely fail without hints. Catching the early signs lets you replace one on a dry day instead of during a storm.

Loud grinding or rattling

Worn bearings or a damaged impeller make the pump louder than it used to be.

Cycles on and off rapidly

A stuck float or a failed check valve lets water fall back into the pit, so the pump restarts again and again.

Runs without moving water

An airlocked or burned-out pump hums but the water level does not drop.

Rust or sludge in the pit

Silt and debris foul the float and clog the intake screen.

Pump is more than ten years old

A typical pump lasts roughly seven to ten years, depending on how often it runs.

Damp smell in the basement

Persistent moisture can mean the pit is not keeping up.

Repair, replace or add a backup

A float switch or check valve is cheap to replace and often restores a pump that is otherwise sound. A motor that has burned out or a housing that has rusted through is better replaced. We give you both options with a price for each so you can decide.

A second pump, or a battery backup, covers the case where power fails during the same storm that fills the pit. Backup systems run off a deep-cycle battery and a separate switch. They take a few hours of runtime, which helps in short outages but not long ones.

Pump types and where each fits

Not every pump in a basement is a sump pump. Matching the type to the job avoids a repeat failure.

TypeTypical useNotes
Submersible sump pumpClear groundwater in a pitSits in the water and runs quietly, most common in homes
Pedestal sump pumpShallow or narrow pitsMotor sits above the pit, easier to reach but louder
Battery backup pumpPower outagesMounts beside the main pump and runs on a battery
Sewage ejector pumpBasement bathroom or laundryHandles solids and sends waste up to the sewer line
Effluent pumpSeptic systems with a separate tankMoves filtered wastewater, not raw solids

Many people say sump pump when they mean a sewage ejector. The two look alike but are not interchangeable, so tell us what is in the basement floor and where it drains.

How we replace or install a pump

  1. Cut power and clear the pit

    We unplug the pump and pump or bail the pit down so we can see the bottom.

  2. Disconnect the discharge

    The union and check valve come off, and we inspect the discharge pipe for cracks or clogs.

  3. Set the new pump

    The pit is cleaned of silt, and the pump sits on a firm base so the float swings freely.

  4. Reconnect and add a check valve

    A new check valve keeps water from draining back into the pit after each cycle.

  5. Pour-test the system

    We run water into the pit until the float lifts, then confirm the pump starts and the discharge runs outside.

What the technician checks on a sump system

A pump is only one part of the system. We check each piece in the path.

  • ✓Pit size and depth, since a small pit makes the pump cycle too often.
  • ✓Float position and travel, to be sure it is not catching on the wall or cord.
  • ✓Check valve direction and condition.
  • ✓The discharge pipe for kinks, ice dams and a clear outlet away from the house.
  • ✓The electrical outlet, ideally a GFCI on its own circuit.
  • ✓Weep holes or a vent hole in the discharge pipe that can cause spray.

What drives the cost of sump pump work

A float swap and a full pit rebuild are very different jobs. We confirm which one you have before quoting.

Pump size and horsepower

A one-third horsepower pump suits many homes. A busy pit or a long vertical lift can call for more.

Pit condition

A cracked liner, silt or a missing pit lid adds work.

Discharge line

Frozen, clogged or crushed pipe has to be cleared or replaced for the pump to work.

Backup system

Adding a battery backup or a second pump is a separate equipment and labor cost.

Storm timing

After-hours calls during heavy rain are priced for the visit, and we say so first.

Water that comes up through the floor from a failed pump is often excluded unless you carry a specific water backup add-on. Check your own policy before a storm, not after.

Plumbing fixes the pump, not the whole basement

A sump pump moves water that is already in the ground around your foundation. It does not stop the water from collecting there. We handle the pump, pit and discharge, and our page on basement waterproofing explains the grading and gutter work that cuts down how often the pump has to run.
