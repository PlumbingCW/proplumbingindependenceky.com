Most toilet problems come from a handful of parts: the flapper, the fill valve, the wax ring, or the bolts holding the toilet to the floor. Below, you will find the symptoms we hear about most and what each one usually means.

What your toilet is trying to tell you

Toilets are simple machines, so a symptom usually points to one or two parts. Here is how to read the most common ones.

Water at the base after a flush

The wax ring or its seal may have failed, or the closet bolts have loosened and the bowl is rocking.

Tank refills on its own

A flapper that does not seat lets water leak into the bowl, so the fill valve keeps topping up the tank.

Hissing that never stops

A worn fill valve or a float set too high keeps water flowing into the overflow tube.

A weak or half flush

Mineral buildup under the rim jets, a low water level in the tank, or a partial clog can all cut the flush short.

Gurgling and slow draining

The problem may sit past the toilet, in the drain or main line, and needs a drain check.

A loose or wobbling seat on the floor

Rocking breaks the seal and can crack the flange beneath. Stop using the toilet until it is checked.

A cracked bowl or tank

Porcelain cracks can seep or fail suddenly, and replacement is the sound fix.

What to do when a toilet is overflowing or leaking onto the floor

Overflow water can carry waste, soak subfloor, and drip into the room below. After-hours calls get the same attention as daytime ones, and same-day appointments are often available if the water is stopped and the situation is steady.

Take these steps while you wait.

  1. Turn the shutoff valve behind the toilet clockwise until it stops.
  2. Lift the tank lid and lift the float to stop the refill, or tie it up with string.
  3. Do not flush, and keep everyone away from the bathroom if wastewater is on the floor.
  4. Put towels around the base and turn off power to any outlet near standing water.
  5. If several drains gurgle or back up at once, stop using water in the house.
  6. Call our 24/7 line and mention if wastewater, not just clean water, is on the floor.

The running toilet that raises your water bill

A running toilet is the most common call we hear, and it wastes more water than most people expect. A flapper that has warped or gone stiff lets a thin stream flow from tank to bowl all day. You may only notice a faint trickle, but the water bill can climb quickly.

A quick check: add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait ten minutes without flushing. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. If the water level sits above the overflow tube, the fill valve or float needs adjusting or replacing.

Both parts are inexpensive, and replacing them takes a plumber a short visit. If two bathrooms keep running, we usually replace the parts in both toilets so you are not back in a month.

Wax rings and flanges: the parts under the toilet

A wax ring is a soft gasket that seals the toilet to the drain opening. The flange is the metal or plastic ring bolted to the floor that holds the toilet in place. If the ring fails, water seeps out at the base. If the flange is cracked or sits too low, the new ring may not seal.

Repair means lifting the toilet, scraping off the old wax, checking the flange, and setting the toilet on a new seal. Rotted subfloor, a rusted flange, or a leaking drain beneath can make this job bigger, so we check what is under the toilet before reinstalling it.

What the technician looks at on arrival

The visit is a short diagnosis before any part is replaced.

  • ✓Confirm the shutoff valve works and the supply line is not leaking.
  • ✓Check the flapper, chain, and fill valve inside the tank.
  • ✓Rock the bowl gently to feel for movement at the floor.
  • ✓Look at the floor around the base for staining or soft spots.
  • ✓Test the flush strength and watch how the bowl drains.
  • ✓Check other drains in the house for gurgling that suggests a line problem.
  • ✓Look for cracks along the bowl, tank, and tank bolts.

What a full replacement involves

  1. Shut off and empty

    The water is closed, the tank is flushed, and the leftover water is sponged out of the bowl.

  2. Pull the old toilet

    The bolts are loosened, the toilet is lifted off, and the floor is scraped clean.

  3. Inspect the flange

    We check that the flange is solid and sits level at the right height for the new bowl.

  4. Set the new toilet

    A new seal goes on, the bowl is lowered straight down, and the bolts are tightened evenly.

  5. Connect and test

    The supply line is attached, the tank is filled, and we flush several times to check for leaks.

What sets the bill for a toilet job

A new flapper and a full replacement are very different bills. These are the details that decide where yours falls.

Which part failed

A fill valve or flapper is a short visit, while a flange or subfloor repair adds parts and labor.

The toilet you want

Standard round bowls, taller elongated bowls, and one-piece or wall-hung models differ in price and in how they install.

Old toilet removal

Hauling away the old unit is part of the plan, and a heavy cast or one-piece model takes more handling.

Condition of the floor

Soft or stained subfloor around the base must be dealt with before a new toilet can sit securely.

Number of toilets

Replacing the parts in several bathrooms in one visit usually saves repeat trips.

Insurance often helps when a failed supply line or overflow causes sudden water damage, but it rarely pays for a worn flapper or a replaced toilet. Read your own policy, since some require proof that the leak was sudden.
