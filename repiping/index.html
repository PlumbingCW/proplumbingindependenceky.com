Repiping in Independence, KY means swapping the water supply pipes inside your walls, floors, and crawl space for new ones. It is a big job, so the first question is usually cost, and the honest answer is that it depends on the house. Licensed plumbers on our team look at the pipe you have, count the problem spots, and give you a clear price before work starts.

Most people call after a second or third leak, after months of brown water, or after a hot-water line that barely trickles. One pinhole is a repair, and our leak detection work can pin down where it is. A pattern of them is a sign the whole pipe is wearing out from the inside.

What drives the cost of a repipe

A repipe is priced on the size of the house and the difficulty of reaching the pipes, not on a flat rate. These are the factors that move the number most.

Size and layout of the house

More bathrooms, kitchens, and laundry hookups mean more pipe runs and more fixture connections to remake.

Access to the pipes

Open basements and crawl spaces are quick to work in. Finished ceilings, plaster walls, and slab floors take more labor to reach.

New pipe material

Copper, PEX tubing, and CPVC have different material costs and different labor needs, which is why a technician compares them for your house.

Drywall and finish repair

Cutting access holes is part of most repipes, and patching and painting them is a separate scope that you can handle or add to the job.

Whole house or partial

Replacing one failing branch costs far less than the whole system, but a partial job leaves the old pipe elsewhere in the house.

Insurance often helps with sudden water damage after a pipe bursts, but it rarely pays to replace pipes that wore out with age. Read your own policy and ask your insurer how it treats a leak that was slow and ongoing.

Rusty water, weak flow, and pinhole leaks: signs supply pipe is wearing out

Pipes fail from the inside long before they fail on the outside. These are the clues homeowners notice first.

Brown or yellow water

Steel pipe sheds rust when water sits overnight. If the color clears after a minute of running, the pipe wall is likely corroding.

Pressure that has faded

Mineral and rust buildup narrows old pipe, so the inside opening shrinks while the outside looks fine.

Pinhole leaks that keep returning

A spot repair holds, then a new pinhole opens a few feet away. That pattern points to thin pipe throughout.

Stains on ceilings or walls

A brown ring that grows after you run a faucet suggests a leak behind the surface.

Hot water slower than cold

Hot lines age faster, and a slow hot tap can mean scale has closed up the pipe.

A slab leak

A damp floor, a warm spot, or a meter that runs with taps off can mean a pipe below the concrete has failed.

Why galvanized and older supply pipe gets replaced

Galvanized pipe is steel coated with zinc, and it was a standard choice for house plumbing for decades. The coating wears away, and rust builds up inside the pipe wall. Flow shrinks to a trickle, the water turns brown, and weak spots eventually leak. Where a fitting joins two pipes, the buildup is often worst. When one of those aging pipes finally splits, our burst pipe repair service handles the emergency part first.

Older copper can also fail, usually at joints or where acidic water thins the wall. CPVC from some manufacturers cracks as it ages. A technician checks each pipe type for its own failure signs rather than assuming one story for every house.

Homes built in different eras may not share the same pipes

Local note

Independence grew by annexation in the 1960s, 1970s, and in 1981, so homes in the city come from several building periods. Houses from different eras often use different pipe materials, and a renovation may have swapped some of them. Do not assume your neighbor's pipes match yours.

The only reliable way to know is to look. A technician identifies the material where the water enters the house and at a few more points, then explains whether a repair, a partial repipe, or a full job fits. What the technician checks to decide between repair and repipe

A repipe should be the answer only when the evidence points there. A visit usually covers these checks.

  • ✓Identify the pipe material at the meter, the water heater, and under sinks.
  • ✓Open a few access points to look at the inside of cut pipe, not just the outside.
  • ✓Count past repairs and note where the leaks appeared.
  • ✓Measure water pressure at an outdoor spigot, then compare it with flow at the fixtures.
  • ✓Check the shutoff valves, since old ones often fail to close fully.
  • ✓Look for splices made of mismatched metals that can corrode where they meet.
  • ✓Trace the main routes through walls, floor, and crawl space to plan access.

How a whole-house repipe goes

  1. Plan the routes

    We map where the new supply lines will travel and choose access points that cause the least cutting.

  2. Shut off and drain

    The water is turned off at the main and the old system is drained so work can begin safely.

  3. Open access holes

    Small sections of drywall or flooring are removed to reach the old pipe, and we protect your floors.

  4. Run new lines

    New pipe is run to every fixture, connected at the main and the water heater, and secured in the framing.

  5. Pressure test

    The system is filled and held under pressure so any weak joint shows itself before walls are closed.

  6. Turn on and flush

    We restore water, flush the new lines, and test each fixture for flow and leaks.

Why a pinhole repair is not always a bargain

A repair fixes one hole, but corrosion works along the whole length of the pipe. If the wall of the pipe near the hole is paper thin, the next leak is only a matter of time. Patching again and again can cost more in repairs and water damage than replacing the supply once. A technician can show you the cut pipe so you can see the wall thickness for yourself.
