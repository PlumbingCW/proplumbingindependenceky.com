A tankless water heater in Independence, KY heats water only while a tap is open, so there is no 40 to 50 gallon tank to run empty. That is the appeal. The catch is that the unit needs the right gas line, vent, and flow rate to deliver it. Our licensed plumbers size the system to your fixtures, install it, repair it, and flush the scale out of it.

Most calls about these units fall into three groups. Some owners want to swap a tank for an on-demand heater and need to know if the house can handle one. Others have a unit that shows an error code, runs lukewarm, or drips. The rest simply want it flushed, not replaced.

Lukewarm showers, beeping codes and cold bursts: what the unit is telling you

Owners rarely call asking for a heat exchanger inspection. They call because the shower fades, a display flashes a number, or a puddle shows up below the unit. Those symptoms all lead back to the same technical subject: an on-demand, or tankless, water heater and the few parts that decide how well it works.

Hot water fades when two fixtures run

Every tankless unit has a maximum flow in gallons per minute. A shower plus a dishwasher can ask for more than the unit can heat, or scale inside the unit can lower that maximum.

A cold blast between hot uses

Hot water sitting in the pipe cools off while the heater is idle, so the next draw starts with a short cold slug. Some of this is normal. A long cold stretch points to a sensor or a flow problem.

An error code with a beeping sound

Most brands use numbered codes for ignition, flame, venting, and temperature faults. The code names a circuit, not a part, so the real cause still has to be tested.

Water that is hot, then not, then hot

Swings in temperature often come from a clogged inlet screen, a failing flow sensor, or mineral buildup that narrows the water passages.

Rumbling or popping while it heats

Scale on the heat exchanger traps tiny pockets of water that flash to steam. The noise is the unit telling you it needs a flush.

Water around the base of the unit

The source may be a loose fitting, a weeping relief valve, a blocked condensate drain, or a cracked heat exchanger. Shut off the water and power to the unit and call us.

What moves the price of a tankless install

Two tankless jobs on the same street can cost very different amounts. The unit itself is only one line. The pipe, vent, and electrical work around it often decide the total, which is why we price after we see the existing setup.

Gas line size and run length

A tankless burner pulls far more gas at once than a tank burner. A half-inch line that served a tank may need to become three-quarter inch, and a long run adds pipe and labor.

Venting type and route

Condensing units use plastic vent pipe and can exit through a wall. Non-condensing units need stainless venting. A longer or more twisted route costs more.

Unit capacity

A bigger household with several bathrooms needs a higher flow rating. A bigger unit costs more and may need a bigger gas supply.

Water-side parts

Isolation valves for flushing, a pressure relief valve, and a condensate drain are all part of a sound install. Adding them to an older plumbing layout takes extra fittings.

Removing the old tank and reworking the space

Draining and hauling out a tank, moving a power outlet, and patching the wall where the old vent ran all add time.

Insurance rarely pays to upgrade a working water heater to a tankless model. If a covered event such as a sudden leak destroyed the old unit, your policy may cover part of the loss, so read it or call your agent before work starts.

Flush and descale: the service most owners really need

Many callers ask us to flush their unit instead of replacing it, and that is often the right call. Heating water drops minerals out of it, and a tankless heat exchanger has narrow passages where scale collects. Over time the unit heats less water, runs hotter, and starts throwing codes.

Northern Kentucky Water District's 2023 report shows about 7 grains per gallon of hardness across its systems. That is moderately hard to hard water. The report does not say which treatment plant feeds your street, so assume your unit sees scale and plan a regular flush. Check the maker's manual for the interval, since many call for about once a year in hard water.

A flush circulates a descaling solution, often white vinegar, through the heat exchanger with a small pump and two hoses for roughly 45 to 60 minutes. We then rinse the unit, clean the inlet screen, and check flame and temperature readings. Units installed without isolation valves need those added first, because the pump hoses connect there.

How an install day runs

  1. Size the load

    We add up the fixtures that might run together and pick a unit with enough flow for them.

  2. Check gas, vent and power

    We measure the gas line, plan the vent route, and confirm a power outlet and condensate drain location.

  3. Remove the old heater

    We shut off gas and water, drain the tank, disconnect it, and haul it out.

  4. Mount and connect

    The unit goes on the wall with isolation valves, a relief valve, the gas connection, and the vent.

  5. Fire it up and test

    We check for gas leaks, run hot water at several fixtures, and confirm the outlet temperature and flame reading.

What a technician checks on a misbehaving unit

A code or a cold shower tells us where to start, not where to stop. These are the checks we run before we recommend any part.

  • ✓Read the code history and match it to the manual's fault list.
  • ✓Test the gas pressure while the burner runs at full fire.
  • ✓Inspect the vent for blockage, loose joints, or a cracked section.
  • ✓Pull the inlet screen and look for debris or scale flakes.
  • ✓Measure the temperature rise between the inlet and outlet.
  • ✓Check the flame sensor and igniter for wear or soot.
  • ✓Look at the condensate trap and drain for a clog.
  • ✓Test the flow sensor with water running at a low and a high rate.

Why gas line and vent size catch people off guard

A typical tank burner draws about 30,000 to 40,000 BTU per hour. A whole-house tankless unit can ask for 150,000 to 199,000 BTU the moment a shower starts. If the old gas pipe cannot supply that, the unit starves, drops its flame, and shows a fault. This is the most common reason a brand-new install underperforms.

Repair, replace, or start over with a new install

A unit with a worn igniter, a clogged screen, or scale buildup is usually worth fixing. A cracked heat exchanger or a repeated fault after a good flush may not be. For a failing tank model that you want to keep, our water heater repair page covers that path, and water heater replacement explains when a swap makes more sense.
