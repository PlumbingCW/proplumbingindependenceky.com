Our licensed plumbers fix what can be fixed and replace what cannot. Many disposals jam on a hard object or trip an overload and need only a reset. Others have a cracked housing or a failed seal and need to come out. We tell you which one you have before any work begins.

Humming, silent, leaking or backed up: reading the sound and the puddle

The way a disposal fails usually tells us what broke. Start with what you hear and where the water is.

Hums but nothing spins

The grinding plate is jammed by a bone, fruit pit, or utensil. The motor is trying to turn and stalling, so shut it off right away.

Completely silent

The overload has tripped, a plug has come loose, or the motor has died. A red reset button on the bottom is the first thing to check.

Water dripping from the bottom

A leak from the base usually means the housing seal has failed or the casing is cracked. That is almost always a replacement.

Water from the top rim or flange

The sink flange seal or mounting ring has loosened. This one can often be resealed without a new unit.

Water from the side or hose connection

The drain elbow gasket or the dishwasher hose clamp has let go. A new gasket or clamp is a small fix.

Sink will not drain at all

Grease and ground food have packed the trap or the line past the disposal. The unit may be fine and the drain is the problem.

Bad smell that returns

Food residue stuck under the splash guard or in the chamber walls rots. Cleaning may solve it, but a worn unit can keep trapping residue.

Water pooling under the kitchen sink right now

A steady leak under the sink is the most common reason for a late call about a disposal. The disposal sits above the cabinet floor, and wiring runs close by. Water and power do not mix, so deal with electricity first.

Call our 24/7 line and describe what you see. We offer same-day appointments when available, and after-hours visits for leaks that will not stop.

  1. Turn off the wall switch, then flip the breaker for the disposal if you can reach the panel without stepping in water.
  2. Close the small shutoff valve under the sink, or the main shutoff if that valve is stuck.
  3. Place a bucket under the leak and pull everything out of the cabinet.
  4. Soak up standing water with towels and run a fan if you have one.
  5. Keep your hand out of the drain opening, even with the power off.
  6. Take a photo of the leak and the unit label for the technician.

What a technician checks before calling it dead

Many disposals come back to life after a few simple checks. We run these before recommending a new one.

  • ✓Confirm the power: switch, outlet or hardwired connection, and breaker.
  • ✓Press the red reset button on the bottom of the unit.
  • ✓Turn the grinding plate with a hex key in the socket at the bottom to free a jam.
  • ✓Look inside the chamber for a stuck spoon, bottle cap, or bone.
  • ✓Check each seal and gasket for seep marks while water runs.
  • ✓Inspect the bottom of the housing for cracks or rust streaks.
  • ✓Run the dishwasher drain hose and check its clamp and high loop.
  • ✓Check the trap and wall pipe for a clog after the disposal.

How we replace a disposal

  1. Shut off the power and water

    We turn off the breaker and the sink supply, then disconnect the cord or wiring.

  2. Detach the old unit

    We unhook the drain elbow and dishwasher hose, then twist the unit off its mounting ring.

  3. Reseat the sink flange

    The flange gets fresh plumber's putty or a gasket so the top seal stays dry.

  4. Mount the new unit and reconnect

    We lock it onto the ring, reattach the drain and the dishwasher hose, and punch out the dishwasher knockout plug if one is present.

  5. Test under real use

    We run water, grind a handful of ice, and check every joint for seep while the motor runs.

When the disposal is fine and the drain is not

A kitchen sink that fills with water is not always a disposal problem. Grease and coffee grounds collect in the trap and the pipe past it, and a stopped line can back up into the disposal chamber. In that case a new unit changes nothing.

If the disposal runs but the water will not leave, the clog sits farther down. A drain cleaning visit clears the pipe, and we can then check whether the disposal needs anything at all. A sink that backs up into a second sink points to the same problem.

What shapes the bill for a disposal job

The cost swings between a ten minute reset and a full swap with new plumbing. These are the real drivers.

Repair or full replacement

A jam clear or a gasket costs far less than a new unit and its labor.

Motor size you choose

Disposals range from about one-third horsepower to three-quarters or more. Larger motors grind tougher waste and cost more.

Wiring and cord

A unit that plugs in is quick. A hardwired one, or one with a damaged cord or switch, takes more time.

Condition of the plumbing beneath

A corroded trap, drain arm, or shutoff valve often needs replacing while the sink is open.

After-hours timing

A call outside normal working hours can carry a different rate than a daytime appointment. We state the price before we start.

Homeowners insurance may cover water damage from a sudden leak, such as cabinet and floor repairs, but it rarely pays for a worn-out disposal. Read your policy or ask your agent before you file.

Why a hex key beats a hand in the drain

Every disposal has a small hex socket on the underside of the motor, usually a quarter-inch size. Turning it by hand with a wrench rotates the grinding plate backward and frees most jams. Never reach into the chamber, since the plate can move if power returns.
